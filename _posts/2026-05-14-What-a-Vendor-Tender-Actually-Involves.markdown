**Leading the Managed IT Services tender this year meant a lot more sitting with a spreadsheet than I expected going in, and considerably less gut feeling than I'd have guessed for a decision this size.**

The starting point was requirements, not suppliers. Before any vendor entered the conversation, I put together what we actually needed from a managed services partner: technical capability across the environment we run, service delivery and response expectations, commercial value, and the quality of ongoing support once a contract is signed rather than just the sales pitch that gets you there. That requirements document became the yardstick for everything that followed, which matters more than it sounds. Without it, a tender quietly turns into whichever supplier gave the best presentation.

From there it became a weighted evaluation, each of those four areas scored against defined criteria rather than a single gut-feel ranking at the end. Weighting matters because not everything is equally important. A supplier who's slightly more expensive but has a genuinely stronger track record on response times shouldn't lose to a cheaper option just because price is the easiest number to compare. Writing the weighting down before seeing a single proposal keeps the evaluation honest, because it's a lot harder to quietly favour a supplier after the fact when the scoring criteria were fixed before anyone pitched.

Supplier presentations were where the paper requirements met reality. A few rounds of clarification questions on pricing structure, what was actually included versus billed separately, and how each supplier would realistically handle an incident outside normal hours. Some answers held up under questioning. Some didn't, and the scoring reflected that.

The recommendation that came out the other end went to senior management with the full scoring behind it, not just a conclusion. That's deliberate. A recommendation that shows its working can be checked, challenged and trusted in a way that "I think we should go with them" never can. It also means the decision holds up later if anyone asks why this supplier and not another, because the answer is documented rather than remembered.

None of this moved fast. It's the same instinct behind every project I take on: build the tool, or in this case the vendor relationship, around what the organisation actually needs, tested and scored properly, rather than picked because a proposal looked polished.
