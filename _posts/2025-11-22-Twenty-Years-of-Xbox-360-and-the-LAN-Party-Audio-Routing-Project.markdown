**The Xbox 360 launched on the 22nd of November 2005. Twenty years to the day, the same group of friends who grew up around that console were back in one room with a stack of them, more cable than any reasonable person should own, and a genuinely embarrassing amount of enthusiasm for a console two decades old.**

This wasn't just another games night. That console is a big part of why this group of friends is still a group of friends, and honestly a big part of why I ended up in IT at all. Long before job titles or qualifications, it was hours spent figuring out why one console couldn't see the others on the network, or why someone's headset had a hum in it that nobody else could hear. Nobody called it networking or troubleshooting at the time. It was just what you had to do to actually play.

So for the 20th anniversary, the brief was simple to say and, as always, harder to deliver: get several Xbox 360s talking to each other for split games, without the audio turning into a complete mess. Anyone who's actually run a LAN knows that's where the real project lives. Nobody wants their own console's menu blips and kill sounds blasting through a shared room speaker while they're also trying to hear a teammate. Everyone needed their own console's audio in their own headset, full stop.

That's the easy half. The harder half was voice. Depending on the game, we wanted either a team channel, so a 4v4 only heard its own side, or a full free-for-all channel where everyone could hear everyone, with the ability to flip between the two without unplugging half the room. So every player's headset ended up carrying two things at once: their own console's game audio, and a shared voice feed from a separate mixer, blended together before it hit their ears.

In practice that meant running each console's audio into a small mixer per player, feeding in a shared line from whatever voice setup that game's mode needed, and taping down more headphone splitters and 3.5mm cables than I want to admit to. Getting one player's audio right is trivial. Getting a room of them right, at the same time, without a ground loop hum creeping in or someone's mic bleeding into a neighbour's headphones, is a genuinely fun puzzle, and one I still enjoy exactly as much as I did twenty years ago.

Playing online is one thing. Being able to actually stand up and yell, or lose it laughing, with the people you're playing against sitting in the same room is a completely different feeling, and it's the whole reason it's worth the cable mess. No headset mic picks that up properly.

Looking back, it's obvious this was the same instinct that ended up paying my bills: a room full of devices that all need to talk to each other correctly, people who just want it to work and don't care how, and a lot of patience for tracing one dead cable through a pile of identical ones. This one was meant as a proper celebration of the console and the friendships that grew up around it, not just another games night, and it's already got us talking about doing it again. Twenty years ago it was just the LAN party that worked properly. Now it's the excuse to get everyone back in one room.
