**Four years in, and this has been the year the work shifted from keeping things running to proving they're run properly.**

Where last year was about the IT Support Coordinator title catching up with the job, this year has been about the job growing into a much bigger remit: governance, audit-readiness and organisation-wide process improvement have become the largest single part of what I do, alongside the infrastructure and service desk work that never stops needing attention.

The clearest example has been building a controlled-document governance framework on SharePoint, Word and Power Automate. Rather than design it in isolation, I sat down with the authors, reviewers and approvers who'd actually use it and mapped how document sign-off already happened before changing anything. What came out of that is a seven-state approval lifecycle with least-privilege permissions and properly governed metadata, proved out in a working prototype before it went anywhere near a wider rollout. It's the same principle behind everything I build: understand the process with the people who use it before you redesign it.

Staff onboarding got the same treatment. Talking directly with hiring managers, building management and HR surfaced just how much of the joiner process was still ad hoc and email-based. That's now a single form that triggers job-code assignment, site-access alerts and IT/equipment provisioning automatically, cutting out a lot of the back-and-forth that used to eat a new starter's first week.

I also led requirements gathering and a weighted supplier evaluation for an organisation-wide managed IT services tender, producing a documented recommendation for senior leadership, on top of the ongoing patch, vulnerability and access-review discipline that audit-readiness depends on. None of it is a single dramatic project so much as a steady shift in focus: less firefighting, more building the documentation and controls that let the organisation prove it's doing things right, not just assume it.

What ties it together is the same lesson I keep relearning every year here: the biggest improvements rarely come from imposing a new system on people. They come from sitting with how something already works, finding where it's fragile, and building the fix around the people who'll actually live with it.
