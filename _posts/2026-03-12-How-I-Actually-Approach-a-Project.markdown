**Someone asked me recently what my actual process is when a new project lands on my desk, expecting a short answer. It isn't a short answer, so here's the long one.**

It starts with a proposal, but not the kind that gets written in isolation and thrown over a wall. Before anything gets drafted I want to understand the problem the way the people living with it understand it, not the way it looks from a spreadsheet. That usually means sitting with whoever owns the process today and watching it happen rather than asking them to describe it, because the two versions are rarely the same. The proposal that comes out the other side pairs with a technical implementation document from the start, so the business case and the how-it-actually-gets-built are never drifting apart from each other.

Then I build something, deliberately small. Not a plan for a solution, an actual working version of it, even a rough one. The document governance framework I put together this year started as a prototype SharePoint site with maybe a tenth of the final permission structure, tested with the two or three people who'd actually be approving documents in it. Same with the onboarding automation: the first version only handled one job code correctly, on purpose, so I could find out what was wrong with the idea before it was wrong at scale.

That testing loop is the part I protect the most, because it's the part most likely to get skipped under time pressure. Stakeholders see the rough version, tell me what's wrong with it, and I iterate before it goes anywhere near a full rollout. I report progress through a plain red, amber, green status rather than a wall of technical detail, because the people who need the update are usually senior leadership who want to know one thing: is this on track, and if not, why not.

Rollout itself happens gradually wherever I can manage it. A pilot group first, feedback collected through a short form rather than word of mouth, then a wider release once the pattern of issues has stopped being new. It's slower than switching everything on at once, and that's deliberate. A mistake made against five users is a conversation. The same mistake made against five hundred is an incident.

The part that's easy to forget, once something's live and working, is writing down what was actually built. Every project ends with a short completion report and, separately, documentation good enough that someone other than me could pick it up and understand it without a handover meeting. Not because I plan on leaving, but because a system that only one person understands isn't finished, it's just a liability with good uptime so far.

None of this is complicated. It's mostly just refusing to skip steps when things get busy, which is exactly when they're most tempting to skip.
